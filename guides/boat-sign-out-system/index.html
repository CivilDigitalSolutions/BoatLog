How to run a boat sign-out system for your rowing club

A step-by-step guide, plus a free printable sign-out sheet template you can start using today.

By Civil Digital, the team building BoatLog · Last updated 2 August 2026

Most clubs start with a sign-out book or a whiteboard by the door, and for a while that's genuinely fine. The trouble comes later — a full boathouse, a new intake of novices, a busy Saturday morning with three squads overlapping — when nobody can say with confidence which boats are out, who's in them, or when they're expected back. This guide sets out a simple system that works whether you're running it on paper or ready to go digital.

1. List your fleet

Start with a definitive list of every boat, with its name or ID and its class (1x, 2-, 4+, 8+, and so on). British Rowing requires boats to display a unique ID code, so use that as the reference point — it removes any ambiguity about which "the blue four" someone means.

2. Decide who can sign a boat out

This is a policy decision for your club and Rowing Safety Adviser, not something a sign-out sheet decides on its own — but once it's decided, the system should make the rule easy to follow. If novices need a coach's sign-off, build a column or a step for that rather than relying on people remembering.

3. Capture the details that actually matter

At minimum: who's going out, in which boat, what time they left, and when they're expected back. Add a notes column for anything unusual — conditions, a substitute rower, a niggle with the boat. Resist the urge to make it complicated; a sign-out sheet nobody wants to fill in is worse than a simple one everyone actually uses.

4. Set an expected return time — and check it

This is the step clubs most often skip, and it's the one that matters most for safety. An expected-return time only works if someone checks it. On paper, that means someone glancing down the sheet before locking up. Digitally, this is where an overdue-outing alert does the checking for you.

5. Decide: paper first, or straight to digital

There's no wrong answer here. A paper system, run consistently, beats a digital one nobody bothers to use. The template below works from day one with nothing to set up. When you're ready for something that works reliably even with no signal at the boathouse, syncs with safety alerts, and can't be left at home by mistake, that's what a boat sign-out kiosk is for.

6. Review it — weekly is enough

A sign-out system drifts if nobody looks at it. A five-minute weekly check — are boats being signed back in, are return times realistic, is anyone skipping the process — keeps it working rather than becoming another abandoned clipboard.

Questions

FAQ

Do we need a sign-out system if we're a small club?

Yes — arguably more so. Small clubs often have fewer boats and fewer people to notice if something's wrong, so knowing who's out and when they're expected back matters just as much, if not more, than at a large club.

Should we start with paper or go straight to a digital sign-out?

Either is fine as a starting point — the important thing is having a system at all. The template above works from day one with no setup. Move to a digital kiosk when you want it to sync with safety alerts, work reliably offline, or stop relying on someone remembering to bring a pen.

Who should be allowed to sign a boat out?

That's a decision for your club and Rowing Safety Adviser, based on experience, supervision and your own risk assessment — this guide doesn't set that policy for you. Whatever you decide, the sign-out system should make it easy to follow.

What if someone forgets to sign back in?

That's exactly the gap an expected-return time closes. On paper, someone needs to actively check the sheet at the end of the session. Digitally, this is where an overdue-outing alert helps.
